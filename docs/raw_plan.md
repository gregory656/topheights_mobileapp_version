# PART I: THE CUSTOMER APP

## 3. The navigation system: every tab has a job

On mobile, I would use a five-item bottom navigation bar. That gives the customer a clear mental map without turning the screen into an aeroplane cockpit.

```

TOP HEIGHTS

[ Home ] [ Shop ] [ Energy Studio ] [ My Energy ] [ Account ]

Home          Your personal energy command centre
Shop          Products, equipment and services
Energy Studio Solar sizing, system design and quotations
My Energy     Orders, projects, devices and service history
Account       Profile, Top Boss Rewards and support

```

The central Energy Studio action could receive a distinctive visual treatment, but the overall design should stay clean. Product categories, filters and deeper tools belong inside their respective tabs.

### Tab 1: Home, the personalised command centre

The first screen should answer five questions:

1. What can Top Heights do for me?
2. What is happening with my current order or installation?
3. How can I reduce my electricity costs?
4. What should I do next?
5. How do I get help quickly?

#### Features 1–20

1. Personalised welcome: “Welcome back, Top Boss” where the customer has opted into that tone.
2. Energy profile: Display whether the customer is exploring solar, has a pending quote, owns an installed system or manages multiple sites.
3. Smart quick actions: Shop products, request a quote, book an assessment or report a fault.
4. Continue where you stopped: Resume an unfinished solar design, cart or quotation.
5. Project progress strip: Show stages such as Assessment, Quotation, Deposit, Installation and Handover.
6. Order tracking: Display order preparation, dispatch and delivery status.
7. Energy savings snapshot: Show verified or clearly estimated savings where sufficient data exists.
8. Recommended products: Suggest compatible equipment based on a saved system design.
9. Personalised offers: Display eligible discounts and financing options.
10. Upcoming appointments: Show site visits, deliveries and technician appointments.
11. Service reminders: Surface maintenance due dates and warranty expirations.
12. Recently viewed products: Restore the customer's browsing journey.
13. Saved systems: Reopen previously created solar configurations.
14. Quick support: Start a support conversation with the relevant order or device already attached.
15. Notifications centre: Collect quotes, payment confirmations, project changes and service alerts.
16. Location-aware service availability: Show supported delivery and installation areas, subject to permission.
17. Energy tip of the day: Provide practical, relevant advice rather than generic inspirational messages.
18. Seasonal energy recommendations: Suggest appropriate solutions based on local weather patterns and actual customer needs.
19. Referral shortcut: Invite another customer and track eligible rewards.
20. Home-screen customisation: Allow customers to prioritise orders, devices, projects or savings.

The flow: A new customer sees the value proposition, available services and a clear call to action. An existing customer sees their actual business relationship with Top Heights. The home screen evolves as the customer progresses from browsing to ownership.

### Tab 2: Shop, the energy marketplace

This is not merely an online shop. It is a catalogue that understands equipment compatibility, installation requirements and the customer's intended use.

#### Features 21–45

21. Solar panels catalogue: Panel models, wattage, dimensions, efficiency and manufacturer.
22. Inverters catalogue: Hybrid, off-grid, grid-tied and other supported inverter types.
23. Battery catalogue: Chemistry, nominal capacity, usable capacity, voltage and cycle-life specifications.
24. Electrical equipment: Breakers, cables, conduits, distribution boards, switches and accessories.
25. Lighting catalogue: LED lamps, outdoor lighting, security lights and commercial lighting.
26. Solar water heating: Available systems and installation accessories.
27. Solar pumps: Pumps, controllers and relevant irrigation equipment.
28. Electric mobility: EV chargers and related equipment, if sold by the company.
29. Energy efficiency products: Smart plugs, efficient appliances and power-management devices.
30. Complete solar kits: Curated bundles with clearly specified components.
31. Product compatibility checker: Flag incompatible voltage, current, battery or communication combinations.
32. Technical datasheets: Downloadable manufacturer specifications.
33. Installation requirements: Identify products requiring professional installation.
34. Stock availability: Distinguish in-stock, low-stock, preorder and unavailable items.
35. Location-specific availability: Reflect warehouse and delivery coverage.
36. Product comparison: Compare specifications side by side.
37. Technical search: Search by wattage, voltage, capacity, model number or SKU.
38. Barcode and QR scanning: Identify products from packaging or an existing installation.
39. Verified customer reviews: Allow eligible purchasers to review products.
40. Warranty information: Display manufacturer and seller warranty terms.
41. Bundle builder: Combine compatible products into a package.
42. Saved favourites: Create personal product wishlists.
43. Price alerts: Notify customers of changes to selected products.
44. Bulk purchasing: Request trade pricing for larger orders.
45. Alternative products: Recommend suitable substitutes when an item is unavailable.

The flow: Select a category → inspect technical details → check compatibility → choose quantity → add installation or delivery if needed → review price → checkout.

A critical detail: the compatibility checker must use verified technical specifications and explicit engineering rules. AI can explain the result, but it should not invent electrical compatibility.

### Tab 3: Energy Studio, the flagship feature

This is the feature I would make the centrepiece of the entire product.

Instead of forcing customers to understand solar engineering, Top Heights guides them through an energy assessment and turns their requirements into a draft system design.

#### Features 46–75

46. Solar readiness questionnaire: Ask about the property, use case, budget and current electricity access.
47. Electricity bill analysis: Allow a customer to enter monthly consumption or upload a bill for assisted extraction.
48. Appliance inventory: Add refrigerators, televisions, pumps, lights, computers and other loads.
49. Appliance wattage library: Provide editable default estimates and identify uncertain values.
50. Daily operating schedule: Capture the hours each appliance runs.
51. Critical-load selection: Identify which appliances must remain powered during outages.
52. Backup-duration selector: Set the desired number of hours of backup.
53. Daytime versus nighttime usage: Estimate when energy is consumed.
54. Solar panel sizing: Estimate array capacity from demand, solar resource and system losses.
55. Battery sizing: Estimate storage from critical loads, autonomy and usable battery capacity.
56. Inverter sizing: Evaluate continuous load, starting surges and applicable design margins.
57. Charge-controller sizing: Assess current, voltage and array compatibility where applicable.
58. System topology selector: Explore suitable grid-tied, hybrid or off-grid configurations.
59. Roof assessment: Record usable area, orientation, shading and structural considerations.
60. Location-based solar resource: Use an appropriate solar-resource dataset for preliminary estimates.
61. Seasonal generation estimates: Account for monthly solar variation where data permits.
62. Energy balance visualisation: Compare estimated production, consumption and storage.
63. Bill-reduction estimate: Estimate potential changes in grid electricity consumption.
64. Return-on-investment calculator: Show assumptions, payback estimates and sensitivity ranges.
65. System design alternatives: Compare budget, balanced and higher-capacity configurations.
66. Component compatibility validation: Check documented electrical and communication constraints.
67. Installation cost estimate: Include labour, mounting, wiring and other applicable costs.
68. Quotation generation: Turn the draft configuration into a formal quotation after business review where required.
69. Save and resume: Preserve an unfinished design.
70. Shareable design report: Export a PDF for a customer, landlord or business partner.
71. Professional review request: Send a draft to an authorised technician or engineer.
72. Site assessment booking: Request a physical survey when remote data is insufficient.
73. Design version history: Compare changes in loads, equipment and price.
74. Scenario simulator: Explore changes in battery size, panel capacity, usage and budget.
75. Design confidence indicator: Identify missing measurements, uncertain assumptions and items requiring site verification.

### Example: the AI sales journey

A customer types:

> I run a small shop and need lights, a fridge and security overnight. I want to reduce my electricity bill.

The assistant gathers the missing information: approximate appliance consumption, operating hours, desired backup duration, site location and budget.

It then uses deterministic calculation services to estimate demand and retrieve suitable equipment from the approved catalogue.

The customer receives three options:

- Essential: Meet the highest-priority needs within a defined budget.
- Balanced: Provide a broader balance of capacity, storage and cost.
- Extended: Explore additional capacity or longer backup.

Each option explains assumptions, estimated costs and limitations. If the requirements are uncertain or safety-critical, the assistant routes the design to a qualified professional.

The rule: AI may explain, recommend and prepare. It must not independently invent stock availability, mark an unverified payment as successful, approve an unsafe electrical design or promise a financing decision.

# PART VII: THE MONEY ENGINE

## 11. How Top Heights can make money

I would not depend on advertisements as the primary business model. An energy company has much stronger options because it sells physical products, professional services and ongoing maintenance.

The most valuable approach is to build multiple revenue streams around the same customer relationship.

### Revenue stream 1: Direct product sales

Sell solar panels, inverters, batteries, electrical components, lighting and other products.

How it works: The company earns the margin between its effective acquisition cost and the selling price, after accounting for applicable fulfilment costs and other expenses.

Useful features:

- Product bundles.
- Cross-selling compatible accessories.
- Trade pricing.
- Volume discounts.
- Stock-aware recommendations.
- Product comparison.
- Abandoned-cart recovery.

Example: A customer buying an inverter may also need compatible batteries, protection equipment and installation. Recommendations should be technically validated, not just commercially attractive.

### Revenue stream 2: Solar design and installation

Charge for site assessments, system design where appropriate, installation, commissioning and project delivery.

Possible pricing structures:

- Fixed-price installation packages.
- Itemised equipment plus labour.
- Site assessment fees.
- Custom commercial project quotations.
- Larger project contracts with milestone-based payments.

This can create substantially more value than selling equipment alone, provided the projects are priced correctly and delivered safely.

### Revenue stream 3: Maintenance subscriptions

Create recurring revenue from customers who want their systems inspected and maintained.

Possible tiers:

| Plan            | Potential inclusions                                              |
| --------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Essential Care  | Maintenance reminders, digital records and priority booking terms |
| Plus Care       | Scheduled inspections and defined service benefits                |
| Business Care   | Agreed service targets, multi-site records and reporting          |
| Enterprise Care | Customised maintenance contracts and service coordination         |

The actual inclusions, prices, coverage areas and response times must be defined by the company. Avoid promising unlimited repairs for a fixed fee unless the financial and operational model supports it.

### Revenue stream 4: B2B and trade accounts

Offer electricians, installers, contractors, property managers and other eligible businesses a dedicated purchasing experience.

Monetisation opportunities:

- Wholesale product margins.
- Volume pricing.
- Contract supply.
- Repeat purchasing.
- Reserved inventory for approved projects.
- Paid delivery or fulfilment services.
- Project quotations and procurement support.

A contractor who orders equipment every week can be more valuable over time than a customer who makes one retail purchase.

### Revenue stream 5: Financing partnerships

Help eligible customers explore financing for equipment and installations.

Potential structures include:

- Deposits plus agreed milestone payments.
- Instalment products offered by an authorised financing partner.
- Asset-financing referrals.
- Financing for eligible commercial projects.

The platform can earn a disclosed referral or origination fee if the applicable agreement and Kenyan legal requirements permit it.

Important: Do not launch an in-house lending or credit-scoring product casually. Lending, consumer protection, privacy and financing arrangements require appropriate legal and regulatory review.

### Revenue stream 6: Top Boss Rewards and referrals

A loyalty programme should increase repeat purchases and referrals without giving away more margin than it creates.

Possible rewards:

- Points on eligible purchases.
- Referral credits after a qualifying transaction.
- Discounts on selected accessories.
- Maintenance benefits.
- Trade customer rewards.
- Rewards for verified customer reviews, provided incentives are disclosed.
- Benefits for customers purchasing multiple systems.

The company must define eligibility, expiry, redemption limits, fraud controls and the cost of each reward.

### Revenue stream 7: Supplier partnerships

Once the platform generates meaningful demand, approved suppliers may pay for legitimate commercial services such as:

- Sponsored placement, clearly labelled.
- Featured product campaigns.
- Co-funded promotions.
- Supplier analytics or campaign reporting.
- Integration and catalogue-management services.

Sponsored placement must never override safety or technical compatibility. A paid product should not be recommended for a system it cannot safely support.

### Revenue stream 8: Paid software for external installers

If Top Heights develops strong installation and maintenance tools internally, the company could later license them to independent solar installers or electrical contractors.

A future software subscription could include:

- Customer and project management.
- Quotation generation.
- Installation checklists.
- Job scheduling.
- Inventory tracking.
- Warranty records.
- Customer portals.
- Reporting and technician workflows.

This is a possible future software-as-a-service business, not something I would build before the internal workflows are proven.

### Revenue stream 9: Commercial energy reporting

Offer businesses structured reporting on energy generation, consumption, maintenance and system performance where data is available.

Potential customers include property operators, farms, hotels, offices and multi-site businesses.

The company could charge for reporting services, managed monitoring or contractual maintenance packages. The value comes from reliable data and useful operational decisions, not merely displaying a graph.

### Revenue stream 10: EV charging and other adjacent services

If Top Heights enters electric mobility or related energy services, the platform could support equipment sales, installation, maintenance and approved partner referrals.

I would keep this modular until there is evidence of customer demand and operational capability.

## 12. Top Boss Rewards: the actual monetisation mechanics

I would separate loyalty benefits from cash-like balances. A points wallet is easier to control operationally than promising that every customer has money they can withdraw.

Here is an illustrative programme, not a proposed final price list.

| Tier             | Qualification example                            | Potential benefit                                 |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------- |
| Top Boss Starter | Registered customer                              | Account, saved quotes and eligible offers         |
| Top Boss Plus    | Meets a defined purchase threshold               | Selected discounts and enhanced service benefits  |
| Top Boss Pro     | Qualifying repeat or trade customer              | Trade pricing and priority quotation handling     |
| Top Boss Elite   | Qualifies under an approved high-value programme | Dedicated account support and negotiated benefits |

The company should decide whether installation value, repeat purchases, referrals or customer lifetime value determine eligibility.

### Example of the reward journey

1. A customer creates an account.
2. The customer purchases an eligible product or installation.
3. The order is completed and any required waiting period passes.
4. The rewards engine calculates the eligible points.
5. The customer sees the pending reward and its conditions.
6. Once approved, the reward becomes redeemable.
7. The customer redeems it against an eligible purchase.
8. The system records the redemption and the cost to the business.

Controls should prevent self-referrals, duplicate claims, rewards for refunded orders and manipulation through multiple accounts.

## 13. Pricing architecture for the business

The company should distinguish between what customers pay and what Top Heights earns.

A useful management view would include:

- Gross merchandise value: The total value of orders processed.
- Recognised revenue: Revenue recorded under the applicable accounting treatment.
- Gross profit: Revenue less the relevant cost of goods or services.
- Contribution margin: The amount remaining after applicable variable costs.
- Recurring revenue: Income from eligible subscriptions and ongoing contracts.
- Customer acquisition cost: The cost of acquiring a paying customer.
- Customer lifetime value: Estimated value of the relationship over time.
- Reward cost: The financial cost of loyalty benefits.
- Service cost per installation: The direct cost of delivering the work.
- Repeat-purchase rate: The proportion of customers who return to purchase again.

For example, a business can sell a high volume of solar equipment and still lose money if installation overruns, delivery charges, discounts and warranty claims are ignored.

The dashboard must help management understand profitable growth, not just impressive sales numbers.

Since the aim is a serious production system, I would favour a modular architecture with clear boundaries. I would not start with dozens of microservices, Kubernetes clusters and expensive infrastructure before the business needs them.

### Why this combination?

TypeScript across the mobile app, dashboard and backend gives the team a common language and reduces context switching.

PostgreSQL is a strong fit because this business is relationship-heavy. Products belong to orders, orders relate to customers, installations have equipment and warranty records, and payments must reconcile with financial records.

NestJS gives the backend a structured approach to modules, validation, dependency injection and access control.

A modular monolith lets the team build and deploy the core system as one backend initially, while separating responsibilities into well-defined modules.

One caution: I would not automatically choose a specific cloud provider or managed authentication service without checking the company's existing infrastructure, data-residency requirements, costs and available expertise.

## 16. Database design: the foundation

A product like Top Heights will become difficult to maintain if it stores every feature in a few giant tables or hides business logic inside frontend components.

| Business accounts   | Organisation, Membership, Branch                               |
| Customer management | CustomerProfile, Address, Lead, Interaction                    |

| Inventory           | Warehouse, StockLevel, StockMovement, SerialNumber             |

| Communications      | Notification, Message, ConsentRecord                           |
| AI                  | AIInteraction, ToolExecution, EvaluationRecord                 |
| Governance          | AuditEvent, DataExportRequest, DeletionRequest                 |

### Five database rules I would enforce

1. Money uses exact decimal or integer minor-unit representations. Do not use floating-point arithmetic for financial transactions.
2. Critical state changes are auditable. Payments, refunds, approvals, stock adjustments and permissions need reliable histories.
3. Use immutable financial ledger entries where appropriate. Correct mistakes through explicit reversals or adjustments instead of silently rewriting history.
4. Use transactions and idempotency for critical workflows. A repeated payment callback must not create a second order or duplicate fulfilment.
5. Separate tenant data properly. If multiple branches, organisations or external installers use the system, every relevant query and mutation must enforce the correct data boundaries.

For solar telemetry, I would avoid storing every high-frequency device reading indefinitely in the main transactional tables. Begin with a sensible retention policy and move to time-series optimisations or a dedicated time-series store if the volume warrants it.

# PART IX: SECURITY, RELIABILITY AND REAL-WORLD ENGINEERING

## 17. Features 276–295: the invisible features that make it production-grade

These may not appear in a product demo, but they determine whether the platform is safe to trust with customer data, payments and business operations.

276. Role-based permissions: Enforce access by role and action, not merely by hiding buttons.
277. Organisation-level data isolation: Prevent one company's users from accessing another company's records.
278. Multi-factor authentication: Require additional verification for sensitive staff accounts and privileged operations.
279. Secure session management: Support session expiry, revocation and device management.
280. Rate limiting: Protect sensitive endpoints from abuse and excessive requests.
281. Server-side input validation: Validate all client input before business logic executes.
282. Secure secrets management: Keep API keys, database credentials and signing secrets out of source control.
283. Encryption in transit and at rest: Protect data using appropriate transport and storage encryption.
284. Payment idempotency: Prevent repeated requests from causing duplicate charges or business transactions.
285. Webhook verification: Authenticate payment and integration callbacks before processing them.
286. Database backup and restoration: Test that backups can actually restore the system.
287. Disaster recovery: Define recovery objectives and procedures for major failures.
288. Centralised monitoring: Track application errors, service health and infrastructure performance.
289. Security audit logging: Record sensitive administrative actions and security-relevant events.
290. Automated dependency scanning: Identify vulnerable libraries and outdated packages.
291. Data minimisation: Collect only what the feature needs.
292. Consent and privacy controls: Record appropriate consent and support applicable data-subject rights.
293. Retention policies: Define how long each category of data is retained.
294. Accessibility testing: Check keyboard navigation, screen readers, contrast and text scaling.
295. Release and rollback controls: Deploy tested releases with a safe way to recover from failed changes.

### The production release checklist

Before the system handles real customers and payments, I would require:

- Automated tests for critical business rules.
- Permission tests for every sensitive operation.
- Payment sandbox tests, followed by controlled production verification.
- Database migration and restoration tests.
- Crash reporting and operational alerts.
- API validation and rate limiting.
- Privacy notices and documented data-handling practices.
- A support process for failed payments, lost orders and installation disputes.
- A documented release, rollback and incident-response procedure.

For a Kenyan business, the compliance review should consider the Kenya Data Protection Act and applicable regulations, the requirements of the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner where relevant, consumer protection, tax invoicing, and the applicable rules for electrical installations and financing. The exact obligations depend on the company's activities and contractual arrangements.

## 18. Features 296–300: the future-ready layer

296. Integration registry: Maintain an inventory of connected manufacturers, payment services, messaging providers and external systems.
297. Public API for approved partners: Expose documented, authenticated interfaces with scoped permissions and rate limits.
298. Feature flags: Enable new capabilities for selected users or branches before wider rollout.
299. Experiment framework: Compare interface changes and offers against measurable outcomes without compromising safety or fairness.
300. Platform health and cost dashboard: Monitor uptime, background-job failures, AI usage, integration health and infrastructure spending.

That's 300 numbered features and capabilities across the product, business operations, intelligence and technical foundations. They are a master blueprint, not a recommendation to build all 300 before launch.

# PART X: DESIGNING THE EXPERIENCE

## 19. The visual identity: premium energy, not generic tech

I would give Top Heights a visual identity that feels like an established energy company with an ambitious technology division.

### Design principles

- Primary canvas: White and soft neutral surfaces.
- Brand accents: Deep forest green or a controlled electric green, with charcoal for text.
- Typography: A clear modern sans-serif with a strong hierarchy.
- Motion: Subtle energy-flow animations, responsive graphs, satisfying progress transitions and animated system diagrams.
- Photography: Real solar installations, technicians, commercial sites, farms and Kenyan homes where appropriate.
- Icons: Consistent, recognisable icons, complemented by actual manufacturer logos and product imagery where authorised.
- Data visualisation: Readable energy-flow diagrams, production charts, savings breakdowns and project timelines.
- Navigation: Five clear customer destinations, with deeper functions organised within each section.
- Accessibility: Good contrast, large touch targets, clear labels and reduced-motion support.

I would avoid gradients everywhere, excessive floating cards and a screen that looks like a collection of unrelated dashboard widgets. Use whitespace, strong typography, clear section dividers and a few meaningful surfaces.

### Signature interaction: the Energy Orbit

Imagine a customer opens My Energy.

At the centre is a simple diagram of their system. Solar panels feed energy into the home, surplus flows into the battery, and grid import is shown when required. As verified telemetry arrives, the diagram updates.

Around it are three useful figures:

- Energy generated today.
- Energy consumed today.
- Estimated savings for the selected period.

The user can tap any figure to see the underlying data, assumptions and time range.

This interaction could become a recognisable Top Heights signature. However, the animated system diagram should degrade gracefully on low-end devices and when data is unavailable.

### Signature interaction: the Energy Journey

Every installation gets a visual project timeline:

`Assessment → Design → Quote → Payment → Installation → Commissioning → Handover → Care`

Each stage has an owner, status, timestamp and relevant documents. Customers see a simplified version; staff can access detailed tasks and approvals.

This makes a traditionally confusing process understandable without relying on dozens of status notifications.

## 22. Journey C: a technician completes a repair

1. The customer reports a problem.
2. The support desk checks the fault category and severity.
3. A qualified technician receives the job.
4. The technician reviews the system history and available diagnostics.
5. The technician prepares the appropriate equipment.
6. The technician visits the site and performs approved checks.
7. The technician records findings, parts and work performed.
8. Required tests and sign-offs are completed.
9. The customer receives a service report.
10. The system updates the service history and any relevant warranty records.
11. The customer receives a follow-up request.

## 23. Journey D: a commercial customer manages multiple sites

A business customer signs in and switches between its registered locations.

For each site, the customer can review:

- Installed systems and equipment.
- Quotations and project history.
- Service tickets and scheduled inspections.
- Available energy data.
- Invoices and payment records.
- Maintenance contracts.
- Reports across multiple sites.

The commercial account should have multiple authorised users, appropriate approval permissions and consolidated reporting. A property manager should not automatically have access to another organisation's financial records simply because they manage one of its sites.

### Phase 0: discovery and validation

Goal: Understand how Top Heights currently sells, installs and supports its products.

Tasks:

- Interview the owner, sales staff, technicians and customers.
- Map the existing sales and installation processes.
- Audit the product catalogue, stock records and price lists.
- Identify current payment, invoicing and communication systems.
- Confirm supported locations and delivery methods.
- Document installation and warranty procedures.
- Select a small number of measurable business goals.
- Agree on the first release and who will approve it.

Deliverable: An approved product requirements document, initial process diagrams, a prioritised backlog and a clickable prototype.

### Phase 1: the commerce foundation

Build:

- Customer registration and profiles.
- Home and Shop.
- Product catalogue and search.
- Product specifications.
- Cart and checkout.
- Payment integration and reconciliation.
- Orders and invoices.
- Basic Command dashboard.
- Notifications and audit logs.

Business outcome: Customers can discover products, place orders and receive reliable updates.

### Phase 2: the energy sales journey

Build:

- Energy Studio questionnaire.
- Appliance and energy-demand calculator.
- Draft solar configurations.
- Quote generation and approval.
- Site assessment booking.
- Project tracking.
- Installation scheduling.
- Document centre.

Business outcome: More structured enquiries, better quotation management and visibility into projects.

### Phase 3: field operations and customer care

Build:

- Technician job queues.
- Site checklists and reports.
- Parts consumption.
- Commissioning and handover records.
- Service tickets.
- Warranty records.
- Maintenance scheduling.
- Offline job workflows where necessary.

Business outcome: Better coordination between office staff, technicians and customers.

### Phase 4: retention and automation

Build:

- Top Boss Rewards.
- Referral tracking.
- Automated reminders.
- Customer segmentation.
- Repeat-purchase campaigns.
- Maintenance packages.
- Management analytics.

Business outcome: More repeat business, referrals and opportunities for recurring revenue.

### Phase 5: connected energy and intelligence

Build:

- Manufacturer and meter integrations.
- Energy monitoring.
- Historical production reports.
- Device alerts.
- AI-assisted product discovery.
- Document extraction.
- Support triage.
- Controlled AI workflow execution.

Business outcome: A more differentiated product with ongoing value after installation.

### Phase 6: expansion

Explore:

- External installer subscriptions.
- Commercial multi-site energy management.
- Supplier integrations.
- Trade purchasing accounts.
- EV charging services.
- Additional regional markets.
- A partner API ecosystem.

Business outcome: New revenue opportunities beyond the company's initial retail and installation business.

The roadmap should be reordered if Top Heights already has an established catalogue, payment infrastructure, field service process or connected equipment.

# PART XIII: THE BUSINESS MODEL IN PRACTICE

## 25. A simple illustrative revenue model

Imagine a future month in which the platform processes the following business. These numbers are purely illustrative, not forecasts or proposed prices.

| Revenue activity             | Assumption                | Monthly value |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------- | ------------- |
| Equipment sales              | 100 orders × KES 25,000   | KES 2,500,000 |
| Installation services        | 10 projects × KES 80,000  | KES 800,000   |
| Maintenance contracts        | 50 customers × KES 2,000  | KES 100,000   |
| Trade software subscriptions | 10 businesses × KES 5,000 | KES 50,000    |
| Total illustrative revenue   |                           | KES 3,450,000 |

That total is not profit. The model still needs to account for equipment acquisition, labour, transport, hosting, payment fees, taxes, warranty obligations, customer acquisition and other expenses.

The lesson is that the strongest business model can combine equipment sales with installation services and recurring care. Software subscriptions are an additional possibility once the platform proves its value.

## 26. The metrics I would put in front of management

The executive dashboard should answer whether the platform is actually improving the business.

Sales

- New qualified enquiries.
- Enquiry-to-quotation conversion.
- Quotation-to-sale conversion.
- Average order value.
- Repeat-purchase rate.

Operations

- Average time to prepare a quotation.
- Installation completion time.
- On-time delivery rate.
- Technician utilisation.
- Rework and return rates.

Finance

- Gross profit by product category.
- Contribution margin by service.
- Outstanding receivables.
- Payment reconciliation failures.
- Warranty and maintenance costs.

Customer experience

- Support response time.
- Issue resolution time.
- Customer satisfaction.
- Referral conversion.
- Maintenance renewal rate.

Technology

- Crash-free sessions.
- API latency.
- Payment workflow failures.
- Notification delivery failures.
- Infrastructure and AI cost per transaction.

These metrics should have defined formulas and reliable source data. Otherwise, the dashboard becomes an expensive collection of numbers nobody trusts.

# PART XIV: THE TECHNICAL DECISIONS I WOULD MAKE FIRST

## 27. My final stack recommendation

For a small team building a serious, expandable product, I would start with:

- Mobile: React Native, Expo and TypeScript.
- Web: Next.js, TypeScript and Tailwind CSS.
- Backend: NestJS with a modular architecture.
- Database: PostgreSQL and Prisma.
- Background jobs: Redis and a suitable queue.
- Payments: M-Pesa Daraja or a suitable aggregator, plus an approved card gateway.
- Storage: Managed S3-compatible object storage.
- Authentication: A managed provider or carefully implemented authentication with server-enforced permissions.
- Testing: Unit tests, integration tests and Playwright for critical web journeys.
- Deployment: Managed infrastructure with CI/CD, backups, monitoring and staged releases.

If speed and a smaller initial budget are more important than architectural control, a managed backend such as Supabase is worth evaluating. It can simplify authentication, PostgreSQL access and file storage, but its access policies, server-side business rules and payment workflows still need careful design.

I would not build custom AI models, a microservices platform, or a complex Kubernetes deployment as the first step. Those choices add cost before the core product has demonstrated demand.

## 28. What I would leave out of the first release

I would deliberately defer:

- Unverified live energy monitoring.
- Predictive maintenance without sufficient device data.
- In-house lending.
- A public marketplace with independent sellers.
- A large supplier advertising network.
- Custom AI model training.
- Blockchain-based energy trading.
- Complex gamification.
- A full software-as-a-service product for external installers.

These are not necessarily bad ideas. They simply depend on business agreements, reliable data, proven demand or operational maturity.

# PART XV: TURNING THE IDEA INTO A REAL PRODUCT

## 29. The first seven things I would do

1. Confirm the actual business model. Establish whether Top Heights primarily sells equipment, installs systems, maintains systems, serves commercial customers or does all four.
2. Map the existing process. Find out how a lead becomes a quote, how stock is confirmed, how payments are reconciled and how installations are scheduled.
3. Create the design system. Define typography, colours, spacing, navigation, component behaviour and accessibility.
4. Build the core database and API. Start with customers, products, inventory, quotes, orders and payments.
5. Implement the customer shopping journey. Make browsing, ordering and payment reliable before adding elaborate AI features.
6. Build the internal dashboard alongside it. Staff need the tools to manage the orders that the customer app creates.
7. Pilot with real workflows. Use a controlled group of customers and staff, measure problems, and improve the product before expanding.

The single most important early dependency is the company's real catalogue and operating process. An impressive interface connected to inaccurate prices, fictional inventory or an unreliable payment workflow will not help the business.
